Tables

Tables use the same language as bar charts and line graphs. The skill is choosing which numbers matter.

1Tables: what is different?

A table is just numbers in rows and columns, without a picture. There are two kinds:

  • Comparison tables — several countries or groups at one time. Use the comparison language from the bar chart lesson.
  • Trend tables — the same items in different years. Use the trend language from the line graph lesson.
Before writing, circle the highest and lowest number in each row or column, and look for anything that goes against the pattern. Those are your main features.

Show a model answer
Pioneer model answer

The table compares Canada, Japan, Peru and Angola in 1994 on four measures: average yearly income, life expectancy, daily calorie supply and adult literacy.

Overall, the two richer nations, Canada and Japan, were far ahead of Peru and Angola on every indicator, and Angola had the lowest figure on all four.

The income gap was the most striking. A Japanese person earned $15,760 a year on average and a Canadian $11,100, roughly 70 to 120 times as much as people in Peru ($160) and Angola ($130). Health showed a similar divide. People in Japan and Canada could expect to live to 78 and 76 respectively, whereas life expectancy was 51 in Peru and only 47 in Angola.

Canadians had the highest calorie supply, at 3,326 a day, almost 500 more than the Japanese (2,846), while Peruvians and Angolans received fewer than 2,000 (1,927 and 1,749). Finally, almost all adults in Canada and Japan could read and write (99%), but the rate fell to just over two-thirds in Peru (68%) and to roughly one in three in Angola (34%).

Sample answer

The tables show how sales of Fairtrade coffee and bananas changed in five European countries between 1999 and 2004.

Overall, coffee sales rose in all five countries, and the UK became by far the largest market by 2004. Banana sales also rose in all but two countries, and were highest in the UK in both years.

In 1999, the largest coffee sales, 3 million euros, were reported in Switzerland, and they doubled by 2004. The figure for the UK was originally 50% lower than that for Switzerland (1.5m), while sales in Denmark were slightly higher (1.8m). However, while the former rose dramatically to 20m, the latter remained relatively stable. There was little difference between the figures for Belgium (1m) and Sweden (0.8m), and both experienced noticeable increases, to 1.7m and 1m respectively.

The UK had the highest banana sales in both years, as they rose more than threefold from 15 million euros to 47m. Swiss sales were originally almost twice as high as those in Denmark (1m and 0.6m, respectively), and both surged, reaching 5.5m and 4m. There was little difference between the figures for Sweden and Belgium (2m and 1.8m, respectively), and both nearly halved, to about 1m in 2004.

Corrected from the slide: the overview said the highest coffee sales were “observed in Switzerland”. That was true only in 1999. By 2004 the UK led with 20m against Switzerland's 6m, so the overview now says the UK became the largest market.
